Heartworms in south Mississippi

Heartworms are parasites passed from one infected animal to another by a mosquito. A mosquito that has previously taken a blood meal from a heartworm-positive animal can pass immature heartworms to dogs and cats by biting them. Both dogs and cats can contract them.

They are especially prevalent in south Mississippi because of the high number of mosquitoes that carry them. The same warm, wet climate that makes the summers what they are makes heartworm a year-round conversation here rather than a seasonal one.

Accurate blood tests determine whether a pet has heartworm disease, and we can run these in-house — often with results while you wait. Medications are available to prevent both dogs and cats from becoming infected, and we stock these at the clinic. If your pet is not currently on heartworm prevention, our doctors recommend bringing them in for an evaluation rather than simply starting a product.

Preventing heartworm disease is always less expensive and more effective than treating it once a pet has contracted these dangerous parasites. Treatment is hard on an animal in a way that a monthly preventative is not.

The puppy and kitten schedule

Puppies and kittens receive some immunity from their mother's milk, but that immunity only protects them for a short period of time. Once it fades they need a course of their own.

They require several booster vaccinations, given three weeks apart, to protect them from viruses common to this area — at six, nine, twelve and fifteen weeks of age. Annual vaccines are recommended one year after the final puppy or kitten booster.

Missing a vaccination can lead to severe illness, which can lead to death. If you are not sure where your pet is in the schedule, call and we will work it out from their records.

Dental disease, explained

Studies show that both dogs and cats need to begin regular cleanings as young as one year of age. It sounds early; the reason is that the damage begins long before an owner can see it.

Without regular cleaning and brushing, pets develop a thin layer of bacteria on their teeth and gum line, which hardens to form tartar and plaque. Left unchecked, the bacteria cause tooth decay and gum disease, making it difficult for a dog or cat to eat most foods.

It does not stop at the mouth. Bacteria there can enter the bloodstream and damage the heart, liver and kidneys, shortening the life expectancy of the pet. A cleaning is important if your pet has a large amount of tartar, has loose or decaying teeth, or did not lose all of their baby teeth.

Caring for a senior pet

Older animals may require blood work to screen for weight-related health problems or the presence of certain cancers. Finding those things early gives you more options than finding them late, which is the whole argument for screening a pet who seems well.

We recommend that any healthy pet visit the clinic at least once each year — to boost vaccinations, to identify health issues your pet may be experiencing without obvious signs, and to monitor their aging process. Pets are good at hiding illness, and a great deal of what we find at an annual exam is something the owner had no way of noticing.

Preparing for a boarding stay

For the safety of your pet and of the others in our care, we recommend that all animals be current on their vaccinations prior to boarding at Columbia Animal Hospital.

If your pet is due for a vaccination booster, or if you are uncertain of their vaccination status, please contact us four weeks in advance of their stay so there is time to get them properly vaccinated.

If your pet requires medication or a special dietary food during boarding, bring it with you. Familiar food is one less change for them to absorb while you are away.
