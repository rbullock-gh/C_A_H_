The proposalHalf a century of practice, and a website that has fallen behind it

Columbia Animal Hospital has looked after the dogs and cats of Columbia and Foxworth since 1975, and has been a small-animal practice exclusively since 2005. Three veterinarians — Dr. Janelle Loper, Dr. Pat Sanford and Dr. Bridgett Williams — are published on your current site, all three of them Mississippi State graduates.

What follows is a proposal for one thing: a new website, already built, that represents the practice the way the practice actually is. Nineteen pages, designed for a phone first, written entirely from your own published words, and built so that search engines can read your address, your hours and your services without having to guess at them.

It is finished. You can open it, read every page and decide. The rest of this document explains what is holding the current site back, what has been built instead, and the three questions I need answered before it can replace the old one.

Nothing on this site was invented.

Every fact on the new pages — the hours, the address, what each service involves, what the doctors studied and when — traces back to something the practice already publishes. Where a source could not be confirmed, the claim was left off rather than guessed at. That record is written down and handed over with the site.

The first screenFour questions, answered before they scroll

Someone looking for a vet is deciding in seconds, usually one-handed. The top of the new home page answers all four of their questions without a scroll.

TodayWhat the current site is up against

None of this is neglect. It is what a site built on a hosted veterinary CMS looks like after a decade of the web moving on around it. Every item below is fixable, and most of them are quick — which is exactly why they are worth listing.

Platform

A desktop-era build

The site is a classic ASP.NET WebForms application — every page ends in .aspx, served from a template shared with other practices on the same system. It works. It was designed for a browser on a desk.

Phones

No mobile-first layout

The pages were not laid out for a phone, and most people looking for a veterinarian are on one. Pinching and dragging sideways to read your hours is friction at the worst possible moment.

Duplication

The whole site exists twice

Every page is also reachable at columbiaanimalhospital.vetsourcecms.com. Search engines see two copies of one practice and split the credit between them instead of giving it all to your own domain.

Security

Indexed on http as well as https

The boarding page and Dr. Williams' page are indexed over plain http. Browsers flag an http page as "Not secure", and http and https count as two different addresses — a third copy of the same content.

Listings

Two directories have the address wrong

The Marion County Development Partnership lists 1498 Hwy 98 East. US Companies lists Old Hwy 98 E. Neither is your address, and local search ranking rests on your address reading the same way everywhere it appears.

Hours

Two aggregators open you at 7:30 on Saturday

Your own site, your Facebook page and three directories say 8:00 AM. Pawlicy and GeniusVets say 7:30 AM. Someone who drives over at 7:30 and finds a locked door does not come back a second time.

Search

Nothing is machine-readable

There is no structured data on the current pages. Nothing states, in the format search engines actually read, that this is a veterinary practice at this address, with these hours, serving Columbia and Foxworth. It all has to be inferred.

Content

The after-hours gap

The site says an on-call veterinarian is available after hours — but nowhere does it say how to reach one. No second number, no answering service, no instruction. That is the most valuable missing sentence on the entire site.

How it looks

White-led and editorial, with near-black sections for weight and two warm accents — an amber and a soft eucalyptus green — so the pages read warm rather than clinical. An editorial serif for headings, a clean sans for everything else. Every color pair was measured for contrast before it was used. This document you are reading uses the same palette and the same proportions, so you can see the tone of it before you open a single page.

What it does that the current site does not

  • Structured data on every page. LocalBusiness and VeterinaryCare markup carrying your opening hours and the area you serve, a Service entry on each of the nine service pages, FAQPage markup on the questions, and BreadcrumbList so search results show where a page sits.
  • One source of truth for your details. The address, phone number and hours are stored once and written into all nineteen pages by the build. Local ranking depends on them matching character for character, and hand-editing nineteen pages is how they drift.
  • Self-hosted fonts. Both typefaces are served from your own site, subset and preloaded. No third-party connection sits on the critical path, and the pages still look right if a font service is blocked or slow.
  • Tap targets that meet WCAG 2.5.8. Buttons and links are big enough to hit with a thumb, verified at phone width rather than assumed.
  • It works with JavaScript disabled. The FAQ uses native browser behavior, the navigation falls back to plain links, and no section can render blank. That is enforced, not hoped for.
  • No sideways scrolling anywhere. Verified at 390px, 820px and 1440px — phone, tablet and desktop.
  • Accessible by construction. Skip link, landmark regions, one heading structure per page in the right order, visible focus outlines, alt text on every image, and reduced-motion support.
  • A validator that runs the whole site. One command checks structured data, that the FAQ markup matches the visible questions, missing files, tag balance, alt text, heading order, broken links, title and description lengths, and address and phone consistency across all nineteen pages. It renders every page in a real browser to check sideways overflow, that every image decodes, tap-target sizes, and that nothing disappears with JavaScript off. It fails loudly instead of quietly.
  • A one-command photo pipeline. Every image slot is built at its real dimensions. Drop your photographs in a folder, run one command, and they are cropped, resized and wired into the right places.
  • A single-file preview you can share. The entire site — every page, both fonts, every image — bundled into one file that opens by double-clicking. Email it, open it on your phone, show it to the staff. It is labeled as a preview so a forwarded copy is never mistaken for the live site.

Two things were deliberately left out. There are no testimonials, because I did not write any — the reviews section carries the practice's own mission statement and a link to your Facebook page, where real clients already leave real reviews. And your Facebook rating is not marked up as a review on the site, because it belongs to Facebook, not to you. Both are easy to add properly once you have first-party reviews with permission to use them.

The point of itWhy this brings more people through the door

No one can promise you a position in search results, and I am not going to. What can be controlled is whether the details are right, consistent, readable by machines, and fast on a phone — and every one of those is currently working against you.

  • The map pack is where local vet searches get settled. Those three results above everything else are driven by your Google Business Profile and by whether your name, address and phone match everywhere they appear. The new site states them identically on all nineteen pages, so the profile finally has something consistent to agree with.
  • Two directories with the wrong address, and two with the wrong Saturday opening time, are costing you visits. Sending someone to 1498 instead of 1409, or to a locked door at 7:30 on a Saturday morning, is a lost client and a poor first impression of a practice that has been here since 1975. Correcting them is on the checklist.
  • Nine service pages give nine things to be found for. Someone searching for pet boarding, a dental cleaning or heartworm testing in Columbia lands on a page about exactly that, instead of a general page they have to hunt through.
  • Your answers can appear in the results themselves. The FAQ is marked up so search engines can lift an answer straight onto the results page — when to spay, when puppies need their shots, why dental care matters — with your practice's name on it.
  • Tap to call, from anywhere on any page. A worried owner with a sick animal does not fill in a form. They call. The number is a single tap from the header, the hero, the footer, every service page and the fixed mobile bar.
  • One clear appointment path. Request an Appointment is reachable from everywhere and lands on a page that explains how booking actually works at this practice, with the phone number front and center. There is no form pretending to send somewhere it does not.
  • Fixing the duplicates consolidates what you have already earned. Redirecting the CMS subdomain, forcing https, and pointing each old .aspx address at its new page carries the ranking the current site has built up over the years across to the new one instead of throwing it away. The full redirect map is written and ready.
  • It is fast on a rural signal. Plain HTML, no page builder, no framework — it loads instead of spinning, which matters more in Marion County than it does in a city.

From youWhat I need before it can launch

Three of these are genuine blockers — the new site should not replace the old one until they are answered — and none of them takes more than a conversation. The fourth is photography.

  1. Dr. Wallace Carson's status

    The current site has a live, indexed bio page for a fourth veterinarian, Dr. Wallace Carson. The brief for this project lists three. He is not published on the new site, because putting up a veterinarian who has retired is a far worse error than a page with three doctors on it.

    Tell me which it is. Still practicing and he goes straight in, with the same treatment as the other three. Retired, and he may still deserve a line in the history — half a century with the practice is worth saying. Either way, that old bio page should come down when the site is replaced.

  2. How someone actually reaches the on-call vet

    The practice publishes that an on-call veterinarian is available after hours, but never says how to reach one. The new site tells people to call (601) 736-3041, the only number you publish, and warns them not to drive over assuming someone is there.

    I need to know what actually happens when that number rings at nine o'clock on a Sunday night, so the site can say exactly that. It is the single most useful improvement available to this website, and it is a sentence long.

  3. Confirm Saturday's opening time

    Your site, your Facebook page and three directories say 8:00 AM. Pawlicy and GeniusVets say 7:30 AM. The new site uses 8:00 AM.

    Confirm which is right, and I will correct whichever listings are wrong as part of the launch.

  4. Photographs of the practice

    Every image on the site is currently a labeled placeholder at the right dimensions. Licensed stock photography is fine for the general care and service pictures, and I can handle that. Two images cannot be stock: the About page and the Contact page both show your building, and a photo of some other clinic presented as yours misrepresents the business.

    A phone photo of the front of the hospital on a decent day is enough to start. Photographs of the team, the surgical suite and the boarding area would all earn their place — real pictures of a real practice beat stock every time, and people can tell the difference.
